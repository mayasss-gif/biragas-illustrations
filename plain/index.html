The rooster and the sunrise

In everyday life

Every morning the rooster crows, and then the sun comes up. Every single morning. If you only ever watched, you might conclude the rooster is doing it.

→
What the scientists call it

Correlation. Two things happen together, so we assume one makes the other happen.

So you flip the switch yourself

In everyday life

How do you find out which switch works the hallway light? You do not stand there watching. You flip one and see what happens.

→
What the scientists call it

Intervention. Stop observing, change one thing on purpose, and watch what follows.

The damp patch on the ceiling

In everyday life

There is a stain on your ceiling. A plumber who replaces the pipe directly above it may be fixing a pipe that was never leaking. The water travelled along a beam.

→
What the scientists call it

The wrong target. Treating the thing you can see instead of the thing causing it.

Four witnesses, four separate rooms

In everyday life

After a crash, a good detective does not take one account. She finds four witnesses and questions each one alone, so nobody copies anybody.

→
What the scientists call it

The four evidence streams: observational, temporal, genetic, interventional.

Where the four stories match

In everyday life

Lay the four accounts side by side. Where all four agree, you can lean on it. Where one disagrees, you write that down too. You do not quietly cross it out.

→
What the scientists call it

Convergence. Independent lines of evidence pointing the same way, with disagreement kept visible.

The pre-flight checklist

In everyday life

A pilot runs 8 checks before take-off. Not a feeling about the plane. 8 specific boxes, each one ticked or not.

→
What the scientists call it

The 8 Bradford Hill criteria: strength, consistency, specificity, temporality, and 4 more.

And the pilot who says no

In everyday life

If enough boxes are blank, the plane does not take off. Not a shorter flight. No flight. You can try it yourself on the next screen.

→
What the scientists call it

The confidence gate. Above 0.8 it is submission grade. Below 0.5 the system returns nothing at all.

The weather man who is never wrong

In everyday life

A forecaster who says 'might rain' every day is never wrong, and completely useless. The one who sometimes says 'I honestly do not know' is the one you trust.

→
What the scientists call it

Abstention. Asked to call 11 leukaemia drugs, it got 8 right and declined on the rest rather than guess.

The chef who cooks the classics first

In everyday life

Before you trust a new chef with an invention, you watch him cook the dishes you already know, and you taste whether he got them right.

→

Benchmark validation across 212 indications and 259 documented pathways, each with a trial number and a citation.
